/free and open project management for everyone/

LucidPlan is a project management for teams of all sizes and jobs.

It's a flexible system made to simplify everyone's life, accessbile to all kinds of users.

If you like my work, please support me by [[https://bmc.link/jjbigorra][buying me a cup of coffee ☕]] so I can continue with a lot of motivation.

* v1.9.0

A ticket is now a place where the work on it happens, not just a card that
describes it.

Time is logged against a ticket, and the log is where the work is written down
rather than a timesheet kept beside it.  Each entry is a duration, the day it
was spent and what was done, and the duration is typed the way people say it:
~1.5h~, ~90m~, ~1h30m~, ~1h 30m~, ~1:30~ and a bare ~90~ are all the same
ninety minutes.  Nothing, an empty field and zero log no time, and a day's worth
is the ceiling, so a typo that turns ninety minutes into ninety hours is refused
rather than stored.  The day defaults to today, the comment is what the entry is
for, and a ticket's total is shown both as ~2h 45m~ and as the ~2.75~ hours a
report adds up in.  An entry belongs to whoever logged it and may be removed by
them -- or by a holder of ~lucidplan:ticket-delete~, since a log that could only
be corrected by its author would stay wrong once that person left -- and
removing one is a soft removal, so the trail still says the hours were logged
and then taken back.

*Sprints* are the stretch of work a team commits to: a name, a goal, a start, an
end and a state (planned, active or closed), reached from the backlog or the
board.  A project works in one sprint at a time, and a sprint's own page lists
what is in it beside the backlog it can be fed from.  Deleting a sprint takes its
tickets out of it first, so a deleted sprint leaves a backlog rather than tickets
pointing at something that is no longer there.

Every sprint has a *burndown*, and it is not kept in a table of its own: it is
replayed from the audit trail the tickets already leave -- a ticket put into a
sprint is recorded as ~sprint-added~, one taken out as ~sprint-removed~, and every
status change is recorded with the statuses it moved between -- so the chart
cannot disagree with the tickets it is about.  It is drawn as SVG in the page,
day by day up to today, against the straight line the team committed to.

A ticket can be given a *deadline*, and its card shows the deadline as a state
-- overdue, due today, due soon (within ~deadline-soon-days~, a week) or
scheduled -- rather than as a date a reader has to compare against a calendar.
A *milestone* is a result the project promised by a date: several sprints may
feed one, and its page keeps a running account of how many tickets count toward
it, how many points they add up to and how many of those are done.

The deadline, the sprint and the milestone are one form on the ticket's own
page, because they answer one question: when is this due, and where does it
count.  All three are optional and all three may be cleared.  A calendar date is
stored as the text ~2026-10-27~ rather than as a number -- it sorts, compares and
is read back by a date field -- and a date that does not exist, the 31st of
February say, is refused rather than rolled into the next month.

Tickets are estimated twice over: in *story points*, the numbers a team burns
down, and in *t-shirt sizes*, which is what a first pass through a backlog is
usually written in.  Both are optional -- an unestimate is stored as NULL rather
than ~0~, so an unestimated ticket reads as one nobody has looked at yet rather
than one worth nothing -- and both are changed in place from the ticket's own
page, next to the status and the type.  The two ladders are ~ticket-points~ and
~ticket-sizes~ in ~lib/lucidplan/domain.scm~, and the menus and the endpoints
are both built from them.

Each ticket carries a *discussion* shaped like a thread: a reply is drawn under
what it answers, indented by its depth.  A removed comment is removed softly, so
the replies that answer it keep their place instead of disappearing with it; its
author may take it back, and a holder of ~lucidplan:ticket-delete~ may moderate
somebody else's.  Bodies are Org mode, as titles and descriptions already were.

*Attachments* are carried per ticket: uploaded from its page, listed with their
name and size, downloadable, removable.  The bytes are stored under a name the
application generates -- a submitted filename is data, never a path -- and
downloads are served by the application, which checks that the attachment
belongs to the ticket in the URL.  Where they live and how large one may be are
~attachment-directory~ (default ~var/attachments~, outside ~pub/~ so it is never
a static file) and ~attachment-max-bytes~ (default 5 MiB).

Every write to a ticket now leaves a row in its *activity*: who, when, which
field, the value before and the value after, one row per field that actually
changed, and no row for a form submitted unchanged.  The trail is append-only.
Beside it the ticket keeps *versions* of its content, one numbered snapshot per
change of content, never rewritten: a version can be read back, compared with
the one before it, and restored -- and restoring writes a new version rather
than erasing the ones in between.

A ticket can finally be *moved between projects* from its own page.  Its
discussion, its files and its assignment move with it and its history stays with
it, because the trail names the ticket rather than the project it was in.  Its
number does not follow: numbers count within a project, so the destination
numbers it as its own next one.

Seven tables are added -- ~ticket_comment~, ~ticket_attachment~,
~ticket_event~, ~ticket_revision~, ~sprint~, ~milestone~ and ~time_entry~ --
together with the columns they hang off.  ~maak migrate~ applies all of it:

#+begin_src sh
maak migrate
#+end_src

An instance that carries attachments has something new to back up besides
~lucidplan.db~: the two are written by different writes and are only together a
complete record.  The README's ~Everything a ticket carries~ section has the
details, and ~attachment-directory~ and ~attachment-max-bytes~ are in ~Instance
configuration keys~.

*Planning poker* is a section of its own, at ~/poker~: a table a team sits at to
put a number on the next thing, and nobody at it needs an account.  That last
part is the point rather than a shortcut -- an estimation meeting is where the
people who are not in this instance turn up, and a tool that asks each of them to
register before they may say "five" is a tool the meeting works around -- so a
seat is a *name and a secret in a cookie*, not a user, and no row of the section
points at ~user~.  A table is a meeting: it is named, it names the first thing it
is estimating, and it picks the deck it estimates with, *story points* or
*t-shirt sizes*.  Everybody else takes a seat by typing a name, plays a card
with one click, and may change it with another; the cards stay face down until
the table turns them over.  Turning them over shows each seat's card and counts
the room out: one bar per card, the card the room agrees on -- or the two it is
split between, because a tie is not an agreement -- the range the votes ran from,
whether everybody played the same card, and the average.  The table is then dealt
the next thing to estimate, and what it settled is kept on the page beside the
questions still to come, so an item the room dealt but never turned over is shown
as unsettled rather than with the cards it was holding.  Closing a table takes it
off the list of tables somebody could walk into and keeps all of it readable.

A table is also the one page several people look at at the same time, so it keeps
itself current: a small state endpoint answers whether each seat has played and
never which card -- there is no field in it for one, so a browser cannot be told
early however it asks -- and the page patches the seat marks in place, re-reading
itself only when the round has moved on or the cards have been turned over.
Playing a card needs no permission at all, which is the point of the section.

The one part of it that does is *writing the answer down*: once the room has
agreed on a single card, a reader whose account holds ~lucidplan:ticket-create~
-- the permission a ticket's own estimate is changed with, and the one the
endpoint behind the panel asks for, so the control is offered exactly when
pressing it would be honoured -- can put that card onto a ticket's estimate, on
the ladder the room dealt.  The write goes through the same column, the same
ladder and the same kind of activity row the ticket's own page writes: the other
estimate is left alone, the change is recorded against the ticket with the
account that made it, and the round remembers where its number went, as a link
beside the question it settled.  A split round has nothing to write -- a tie is
not an agreement -- and neither has a round nobody played.

A table can also be opened *for* a ticket, from that ticket's own page, and is
then about it for as long as it stands: it is named after the ticket, its first
round asks the ticket, every round it deals is estimated for it, and the push at
the end is one button naming the ticket rather than a number typed into it.  A
table opened for nothing -- from the list of tables -- is asked where its answer
goes, as it always was.  Which ticket a table is for is the room's own two
columns, and where a round's number went is still the round's own: the two are
not the same fact, and a table whose ticket has since been deleted says so
instead of offering a button that would be refused.  Opening a table for a ticket
asks ~lucidplan:ticket-create~, the permission the estimate it will hold is
changed with; playing a card at it still does not.

Four tables come with all of this -- ~poker_room~, ~poker_item~,
~poker_participant~ and ~poker_vote~ -- together with the two columns a settled
round keeps to say which ticket took its number, the two a table keeps to say
which ticket it was opened for, and ~maak migrate~ applies all of it the same
way.

A ticket's own page was laid out again, since it is where the work on a ticket
is read as much as written.  The card and the panels under it -- the plan, the
hours the ticket has cost, the discussion, the files, the activity and the
versions -- stand in one column of a readable width rather than stretched across
the screen; the labels above the controls are quiet enough to read past; and
what a reader does with a ticket -- asking the team for an estimate, changing
it, deleting it -- is one line at the foot of the card instead of a column of
its own.  A comment is drawn as the card its replies hang under rather than as a
paragraph with a number over it, and a panel's heading is ruled off from the
rows it opens, which is what makes a panel of many rows read as one section of a
ticket rather than as a run of cards.  Every stamp the ticket's own record keeps
-- in the activity trail, under a comment and on a version alike -- is shown as
the date and the time of day it happened, ~2026-10-27 18:14~, instead of as the
epoch second the schema stores.

~maak i18n-update-template~ was fixed: it passed an unmatched glob for
~lib/lucidplan/job/~ to xgettext, which then stopped without writing the
template at all.  A directory that is not there is now left out of the list
rather than passed on.

* v1.8.0

New tickets are announced by email. When a ticket is created, [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/pingwing][pingwing]] is asked for the ~lucidplan-new-ticket~ template and it goes to everyone who takes part in the project -- every user the project has assigned a ticket to, the ticket's assignee and the person who opened it. A message counts as sent only when the service answers that it took it: a service that cannot be reached, or that refuses the task, is retried, logged with what it said, and never fails the write. See ~What the mail service is asked to send~ in the README.

Migrations are applied by mutastructura through ~maak migrate~ (and by ~maak init-db~ on a fresh database), and are tracked in a ~migrations_history~ ledger. The hand-rolled ~for f in resources/migrations/sqlite/*.sql; do sqlite3 ...~ loop is gone. *A database that was migrated by hand needs its ledger written once*, by recording the migrations already applied, before ~maak migrate~ is used on it; the README's ~The database~ section has the commands.

Tests are run with veritas -- ~maak test~ -- and live under ~test/veritas/~, with ~unit/~ and ~benchmark/~ beneath it.

Added ~ticket_statuses~ column to the ~project~ table:

#+begin_src sh
sqlite3 database.db 'ALTER TABLE "project" ADD COLUMN "ticket_statuses" varchar(4096)'
#+end_src

The statuses a ticket moves through are no longer fixed in the code. An instance sets ~ticket-statuses~ in its ~env.scm~ to rename, add, remove and reorder them, and any project may override the whole list for itself from its own edit page. The README has a section on both, ~Renaming or changing the ticket statuses~ and ~A project with its own statuses~.

What the front page says about the software -- the two headings, the lines under them and the feature cards -- moved into configuration too, as ~landing-features-title~, ~landing-features-intro~, ~landing-features~, ~landing-workflow-title~ and ~landing-workflow-intro~.
