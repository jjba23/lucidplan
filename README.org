/free and open project management for everyone/

LucidPlan is a project management system for teams of all sizes and all kinds
of work: a multi-project workspace, a priority-ordered backlog, and drag-and-drop
kanban boards, kept deliberately simple and hackable end to end in Scheme.

* What LucidPlan is
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: what-lucidplan-is
:END:

LucidPlan enhances the use of Agile work methodologies in teams thanks to its
fast-paced, no-nonsense workflows and deep customizability, also thanks to being
written in Lisp.

This tool is the fruit of years of experience using bloated proprietary systems
like Jira and Trello, and experiencing the insane frustrations they bring.

** Who it is for
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: who-it-is-for
:END:

- *Small teams* that want a board and a backlog today, not a six-week rollout.
- *Dozens of projects at once*: one workspace, one dashboard, one search box.
- *Lisp people*, who can make the thing behave differently by writing a few
  lines of Scheme instead of filing a feature request.
- *Self-hosters and freedom-lovers*, who want their team's work on their own
  machine, under their own licence, without an account anywhere else.

** What it is built with
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: what-it-is-built-with
:END:

Built with [[https://www.gnu.org/software/guile/][Guile Scheme]] and [[https://artanis.dev/][GNU Artanis]], [[https://sqlite.org/][SQLite]], [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/olive-css][Olive CSS]] and some small sprinkles of vanilla JavaScript.

* Features
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: features
:END:

LucidPlan provides a comprehensive suite of tools to keep your projects
organized:

- *Multi-Project Workspaces*: Easily manage dozens of repositories or projects
  from a single unified dashboard. Features a quick-access "go to" dropdown and
  search functionality to navigate between contexts instantly.
- *Versatile Backlog Management*: View all your project tasks in a clean,
  list-based backlog with priority drag and drop. Tickets have different types
  (=Task=, =Bug=, =Story=, =Spike=, =Time Box=), configurable statuses, org-mode
  syntax descriptions with links and rich content, and easy editing/deletion.
- *Kanban Boards*: Visualize your workflow with built-in Kanban boards.
  Effortlessly track tickets and drag and drop as they move through columns like
  =To-do=, =Work in Progress=, and =In Review=.
- *Clean and Distraction-Free UI*: A minimalist and accessible interface built
  with [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/olive-css][Olive CSS]], featuring global search, quick project creation, and a
  toggleable light/dark mode to suit your preferences.
- *Granular User Management*: Add, edit, and manage team members with ease.
  Permissions are handled using native Lisp lists (e.g.,
  ~`(lucidplan:ticket-create lucidplan:project-delete)~), allowing for
  incredibly fine-grained, programmatic access control, effectively providing an
  easy to use [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access-control_list][ACL]] with which you can control exactly what each user can/cannot
  do.
- *Org mode support*: Titles and descriptions can be written using Org mode
  syntax, which enables more advanced media. This Org text gets automatically
  rendered to attractive [[https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/][HTML]].
- *Flexible statuses*: the statuses a ticket moves through are configuration,
  not code -- rename them, drop the ones your team does not use, add your own,
  or give a single project its own board.
- *Estimates*: every ticket can be estimated twice over -- in story points, the
  numbers a team burns down, and in t-shirt sizes, which is what a first pass
  through a backlog is usually written in.  Both are optional and both are
  changed from the ticket itself, so an unestimated ticket reads as one nobody
  has looked at yet rather than as one worth nothing.
- *Threaded discussion*: a ticket carries a conversation, not a comment box.
  A reply is drawn under what it answers, indented by its depth, so a thread
  reads the way the discussion went; a comment that is removed keeps its place
  in that thread instead of taking the replies to it down with it.
- *Attachments*: files are carried by the ticket they belong to, uploaded from
  its page and downloaded through the application, which checks that the caller
  named a file of the ticket in the URL.  Nothing a browser sends is ever used
  as a path on disk.
- *An audit trail and versions*: every write to a ticket is recorded -- who did
  it, when, the field, the value before and the value after -- and every change
  of content is kept as a numbered version that can be read back and restored.
  A version is never rewritten, so restoring an old one adds a new version
  whose content happens to be the old content.
- *Moving a ticket between projects*: a ticket filed in the wrong place is one
  menu away from the right one.  Its discussion, its files, its assignment and
  the hours logged against it move with it, and its history stays with it too,
  because the trail names the ticket rather than the project it was in.
- *Time logging*: every ticket keeps a log of the hours spent on it -- a
  duration, the day it was spent, and what was done -- written the way people
  say it, so an hour and a half is =1.5h=, =90m=, =1h30m=, =1h 30m= or =1:30=
  and all of them are the same ninety minutes.  The day defaults to today, the
  comment is what the entry is for, and a ticket's total reads both as =2h 45m=
  and as the =2.75= hours a report adds up in.
- *Sprints and a burndown*: a project commits to a stretch of work by giving it
  a start, an end and a goal, and the tickets it holds carry its id.  Each
  sprint has its own page with a burndown drawn from the audit trail the
  tickets already leave -- what was left, day by day, against the line the team
  committed to -- so the chart cannot disagree with the tickets it is about.
- *Deadlines and milestones*: a ticket can be due on a day, which its card
  shows as a state -- overdue, due today, due soon, or scheduled -- rather than
  as a date a reader has to compare against a calendar.  A milestone is a
  result the project promised by a date, several sprints may feed one, and the
  page keeps a running account of how much of each is done.
- *Planning poker*: a table a team sits at to put a number on the next thing,
  and nobody at it needs an account.  Somebody opens a meeting, names what is
  being estimated and chooses how the room estimates -- story points or t-shirt
  sizes -- and everybody else takes a seat by typing a name.  Every card stays
  face down until the table turns them over, and what the room said is counted
  out beside the seats; whoever may edit the backlog can then write the card the
  room agreed on onto the ticket it was estimated for, which is the one part of
  the section that needs an account.
- *Email notifications*: a new ticket is announced to everyone taking part in
  the project.
- *A [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representational_state_transfer][REST]] [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/API][API]]*: =/api/v1= hands tickets and projects to your scripts, with the
  same permissions the web interface uses.
- *Fully Customizable*: Everything an instance says about itself -- its name,
  its description, its footer, the address mail is sent from -- is
  configuration, and the behaviour and content of everything else can be hacked
  with simple Lisp (Scheme) code.
- *Cross-platform*: Runs anywhere that Guile Scheme can run, and exposes web
  endpoints, and is thus accessible from computers, phones, etc.
- *Free Software*: Contribute to its development or extend it with your own
  custom features.

* Using LucidPlan
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: using-lucidplan
:END:

** Backlog Management
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: backlog-management
:END:

LucidPlan provides several ways to organize and work with tickets directly from
the web interface. Ticket management is designed around fast, simple
interactions.

Tickets in the backlog can be reordered (in terms of priority) using native
browser drag-and-drop. A rearrangement is written as the tickets' priorities, so
the grip is offered while the backlog is sorted by priority -- ~Priority -
Descending~ or ~Priority - Ascending~ -- and each direction writes the numbers
that keep the rows where they were put. Under any other sort the row would slide
back to its old place on the next visit, so no grip is offered there; pick a
priority sort to reorder.

Backlog tickets can be expanded and collapsed without navigating away from the
page, singly or all at once with *Expand All* and *Collapse All*.

Each ticket contains an expandable details section containing information such
as:

- description;
- status;
- type;
- priority;
- story-point and t-shirt-size estimates;
- assignee;
- project/ticket identifier;
- edit and delete actions.

The backlog also carries a one-glance summary of the project -- how many tickets
are open, done and rejected, how many there are in total, and a completion
bar -- plus a sort menu and a search box.

** Kanban Management
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: kanban-management
:END:

The Kanban board also provides a drag-and-drop interaction. Dragging a ticket
onto another Kanban column changes its status.

For example, a ticket can be moved from:

#+name: status-flow
#+begin_example
To-do → Work in Progress → In Review → Done
#+end_example

** Everything a ticket carries
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: everything-a-ticket-carries
:END:

Opening a ticket shows the ticket itself and, under it, everything the work on
it has gathered: the plan it sits in, the hours it has cost, its estimates, its
discussion, its files, what has happened to it and the versions of what it said.
All of it is written by the same permission the ticket is:
~lucidplan:ticket-create~.

*** Estimates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: estimates
:END:

A ticket is estimated twice over, because the two answer different questions:

- *story points* -- the numbers a team burns down, taken from a ladder
  (~ticket-points~, by default 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21 and 34);
- *t-shirt sizes* -- ~XS~, ~S~, ~M~, ~L~, ~XL~ and ~XXL~, which is what a first
  pass through a backlog is usually written in.

Both are optional, and an unestimated ticket is a ticket nobody has looked at
yet rather than a ticket worth zero: an unestimate is stored as nothing at all,
never as ~0~, and the ticket reads as *unestimated* until somebody says
otherwise.

Both are changed in place, from the ticket's own page, by the same controls the
status and the type are changed with -- and both can be cleared again, which is
the first row of each menu.  They are also part of the ticket's edit form, and
they are part of what a version snapshots (see [[#activity-and-versions][Activity and versions]]).

The two ladders are ~ticket-points~ and ~ticket-sizes~ in
~lib/lucidplan/domain.scm~; the menus a page offers and the endpoints behind
them are both built from those two lists, so a team that estimates in powers of
two edits one line and the whole instance follows.  Whatever is not on the
ladder is refused rather than stored.

*** Logging time
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: logging-time
:END:

A ticket keeps a log of the hours it has cost.  Each entry is three things:

- *how long* -- written the way people say it.  An hour and a half can be typed
  as =1.5h=, =90m=, =1h30m=, =1h 30m=, =1:30= or a bare =90=, and every one of
  them is stored as the same ninety minutes.  What cannot be a duration is
  refused rather than guessed at: nothing, an empty field and zero log no time,
  and a day's worth (24 hours) is the ceiling, so a typo that turns 90 minutes
  into 90 hours is refused instead of stored;
- *which day* -- a date, defaulting to today, because "two hours on this" is
  said about today far more often than about any other day;
- *what was done* -- the comment that makes the entry a record of work rather
  than a timesheet.  It is stored as it was typed and shown as text, so nothing
  in it is markup.

The panel shows the entries newest day first, each with its author, its day and
its comment, and the ticket's total -- both as a duration (=2h 45m=) and as the
decimal hours a report is written in (=2.75=).  An entry belongs to whoever
logged it, and that is who may remove it; a holder of ~lucidplan:ticket-delete~
may remove another person's too, because a log that could only be corrected by
its author would stay wrong forever once that person left.  Removing an entry is
a soft removal: the row stays, so the trail still says the hours were logged and
then taken back, and the total simply stops counting them.

The durations are read and written by ~parse-duration~, ~format-duration~ and
~format-hours~ in ~lib/lucidplan/domain.scm~, and the ceiling is
~time-entry-max-minutes~ beside them.

*** The plan: deadline, sprint and milestone
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: the-plan
:END:

A ticket's *plan* is one small form on its own page, because the three things in
it answer one question: when is this due, and where does it count.

- *deadline* -- the day the ticket is due.  It is shown as a state rather than
  as a date, because a date is something a reader has to compare against a
  calendar and a state is something they can act on: *overdue*, *due today*,
  *due soon* (within ~deadline-soon-days~, a week) or *scheduled*.  The
  threshold is one value in ~lib/lucidplan/domain.scm~, so every badge and every
  sentence about a deadline agrees about what "soon" means;
- *sprint* -- the stretch of work the ticket is being worked in (see
  [[#sprints-and-burndown][Sprints and burndown]]);
- *milestone* -- the result the ticket counts toward (see
  [[#milestones][Milestones]]).

All three are optional and all three may be cleared, so the form always posts
all three and what it says is written as it stands.  A sprint or a milestone is
accepted only when it names one the project actually has -- a ticket pointing at
a sprint that does not exist would be a ticket no board could show -- and the
empty row stores nothing rather than a reference to nothing.

A calendar date is stored as the text =2026-10-27= rather than as a number,
because that is what sorts, compares and can be read back by a date field.  What
a form leaves empty is the absence of a date, which is a state of its own: a
sprint nobody has dated, a milestone nobody has promised a day for.  A date that
is submitted but does not exist -- the 31st of February, say -- is refused
rather than rolled into the next month.

*** Discussion
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: discussion
:END:

A ticket carries a conversation, and the conversation is a thread: any comment
can be answered, and the answer is drawn under what it answers, indented by its
depth, so a discussion reads in the shape it happened in rather than as one
long list.

Comment bodies are written in Org mode syntax, exactly as titles and
descriptions are, and are rendered to HTML when the page is drawn.

Removing a comment is a *soft* removal, and that is deliberate: a reply whose
parent disappeared would lose its place in the thread, so the removed comment
stays where it is as an empty node -- shown as ~[removed]~ -- and the replies
to it keep hanging from it.  A comment may be removed by whoever wrote it, or
by anyone holding ~lucidplan:ticket-delete~, and the removal is recorded in the
ticket's history either way.

*** Attachments
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: attachments
:END:

Files are uploaded from the ticket's own page and listed there with the name
they were uploaded under and their size.  A file can be downloaded and removed
again by anyone who may edit the ticket.

What a browser sends as a filename is treated as data and never as a path: the
bytes are stored under a name the application generates itself, and the
submitted name is kept in the database and cleaned before it is written into a
download header.  A file therefore cannot escape the directory it is kept in,
however it was named.

Downloads are answered by the application rather than by the static file
server, so the attachment id has to name a file *of the ticket in the URL*: a
file cannot be fetched by guessing a name in the upload directory.

How large a file may be is configuration -- ~attachment-max-bytes~, 5 MiB by
default -- and a file over that is refused with the ticket's own page, saying
so beside the control it was chosen with, rather than with a bare error page.
Nothing is written to a directory on the way: the bytes go into the
attachment's own row, so a copy of ~lucidplan.db~ is a copy of everything a
ticket carries.

That ceiling is the application's, and it has to stay below the one Artanis
puts on a request body (~upload.size~ in ~conf/artanis.conf~, 8 MiB here) --
the framework refuses a larger body before it reads it, and its refusal is a
response a browser cannot finish reading.  Raising the application's ceiling
therefore means raising the framework's with it, or the reader meets a request
that appears to hang instead of a file that was refused.

*** Activity and versions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: activity-and-versions
:END:

Every write the application makes to a ticket leaves a row in the ticket's
*activity*: who did it, when, and -- when it was a change to a field -- the
field, the value before it and the value after it.  Creating, editing,
assigning, commenting, attaching, restoring and moving are all in there, one
row per field that actually changed, so a form submitted without a change
writes no history at all.  The trail is append-only: nothing in the application
updates or removes a row, because a history that can be rewritten is not an
audit trail.

Every stamp in that record -- the trail's own, the one under a comment, and the
two a version shows -- is read as the date and the time of day it happened,
~2026-10-27 18:14~, rather than as the epoch second the schema keeps it in.  A
stamp is said the same way wherever it is said, so a page of them reads as one
page (see ~recorded-at~ in ~app/views/tickets.scm~).

What is watched is one list -- ~ticket-tracked-fields~ in
~lib/lucidplan/domain.scm~ -- so the trail and the versions cannot quietly
disagree about what a ticket is made of.  It holds the title, the description,
the status, the type, the priority and both estimates.  The ticket's "last
edited" stamp is deliberately not on it: it moves on every write and says
nothing about what the ticket means.

Alongside the trail the ticket keeps its *versions*: every time its content
changes, the content it now has is written down as a numbered snapshot, and no
snapshot is ever rewritten.  Opening a version shows what changed between it
and the one before it, and restoring an old one is an ordinary edit that
happens to write the old content -- so the versions in between stay where they
are, and the history says a restore happened rather than pretending it never
did.  Comments and attachments are not snapshotted: they are records of their
own, and the trail already says when they were added.

*** Moving a ticket between projects
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: moving-a-ticket-between-projects
:END:

A ticket filed in the wrong project is moved from its own page: the ticket
offers the other projects, and choosing one moves it in a single step.

What follows the ticket is everything that belongs to it rather than to the
project: its discussion, its files and its assignment.  What stays is its
history -- the trail and the versions name the ticket, not the project it was
in, so they are read back unchanged and the ticket arrives with its whole past.

Its number does not follow it.  Ticket numbers count within a project, so the
destination numbers the moved ticket as its own next one, and the page you land
on is the ticket's new address.  A status the destination's board does not name
needs no thought either: such a ticket is treated as open, so it appears on the
new board and can be dragged onto a status that exists there.

** Sprints and burndown
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: sprints-and-burndown
:END:

A sprint is a stretch of work a team commits to.  It has a name, a goal, a
start, an end and a state, and it is reached from the backlog or the board by
the calendar button at the top of either.

A sprint is in one of three states:

#+name: sprint-states
#+begin_example
planned → active → closed   (and closed → planned, to reopen one)
#+end_example

A project works in at most one sprint at a time: starting a sprint puts any
other running one back to *planned*.  It is not closed -- closing is the team's
own statement that the work is done -- it simply stops claiming to be the sprint
the team is in.

The tickets of a sprint are the tickets that carry its id.  A sprint's own page
lists what is in it and, beside it, the backlog it can be fed from: adding a
ticket takes one press, and taking one out leaves it exactly as it was, only no
longer in a sprint.  Deleting a sprint takes its tickets out of it first, for
the same reason -- a deleted sprint leaves a backlog, never a set of tickets
pointing at something that is no longer there.

*** The burndown

A sprint's page draws its burndown: what was left to do at the end of each day,
against the straight line the team committed to, so the gap between the two
lines says at a glance what a table of the same numbers would take a minute to
say.

The chart is not kept in a table of its own.  It is replayed from the audit
trail the tickets already leave -- a ticket put into a sprint is recorded as
~sprint-added~, one taken out as ~sprint-removed~, and every status change is
recorded with the statuses it moved between -- so the chart cannot disagree with
the tickets it is about, and there is no second copy of the truth to fall out of
step with the first.

Two things are taken as they are now rather than as they were then, and they are
worth knowing when reading the chart: a ticket's estimate is the estimate it
carries today, and a ticket nothing recorded an entry for is taken to have been
in the sprint from the day the sprint began.

The chart stops at today: a burndown has no business drawing the future as
though it had happened.  A closed sprint is drawn to its last day.

The numbers behind it come from ~burndown-series~ in
~lib/lucidplan/domain.scm~, which answers one row per day -- the scope, what was
done and what is left -- and ~burndown-summary~, which says the last of those
rows once for a reader who does not want to read a chart.  The chart itself is
SVG drawn into the page, so it needs no script and no image to be fetched.

** Milestones
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: milestones
:END:

A milestone is a result the project promised by a date.  It is not a sprint and
is deliberately not shaped like one: a sprint is a promise the team makes to
itself about a stretch of work, while a milestone is a promise made outside the
team about a result -- which is why a milestone has a date of its own, and why
several sprints may feed one.

The milestones page lists them soonest first, with each one's date read the way
a deadline is read (past, today, soon, ahead), and with a running account of the
tickets that count toward it: how many, how many points they add up to, and how
many of those are done.

A milestone is reached from the backlog or the board, by the target button at
the top of either.  Deleting a milestone leaves its tickets where they are --
they simply no longer count toward anything -- for the same reason deleting a
sprint does: a milestone is a promise about the work, and withdrawing the
promise does not withdraw the work.

** Planning poker
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: planning-poker
:END:

Estimating a backlog is a conversation, and planning poker is how most teams
have it: everybody puts a card down at the same time, the cards are turned over
together, and the two people who played a three and a thirteen get to explain
themselves before anybody averages anything.  The section is at *poker* in the
header.

Nobody at a table needs an account, and that is the point rather than a
shortcut.  An estimation meeting is where the people who are not in this
instance turn up -- a product owner, somebody from another team, a guest for the
afternoon -- and a tool that asks each of them to register before they may say
"five" is a tool the meeting works around.  So a seat is a name somebody typed
and a secret their browser holds in a cookie: type a name, press *sit down*, and
you are at the table.  Nothing else is asked for, and the page says so.

A *table* is a meeting.  Opening one names it -- "Sprint 12 planning" -- names
the first thing it is estimating, and picks the deck it estimates with: *story
points*, the numbers a team burns down, or *t-shirt sizes*, which is what a first
pass through a backlog is usually written in.  The deck belongs to the meeting
and not to the round, because a team that estimates in t-shirt sizes does so all
afternoon, and a room that changed its ladder halfway through would leave its
own record of what was agreed unreadable.  The *tables you could sit at* list
shows the meetings still open with the question each is asking, how many rounds
it is on, how many people have taken a seat, and which ticket the meeting is
about where it was opened for one.

A table is often opened for one thing, and a ticket's own page offers to do
exactly that: *estimate with a table*, with the ladder to deal -- the one the
ticket is already estimated on where it is estimated on one, and story points
otherwise.  A table opened that way is about that ticket and nothing else.  It is
named after the ticket, its first round asks the ticket, and every round it is
dealt is estimated for it, so nobody types a ticket number at the end: the push
is one button naming the ticket, and no round can be written onto a ticket its
table was not opened to estimate.  The table says which ticket it is about -- in
its heading, and in the list of tables -- and both are links back to it.  Opening
one asks for ~lucidplan:ticket-create~, the permission a ticket's estimate is
changed with, because it is deciding where that estimate will come from; sitting
at it and playing a card still needs no account at all.

Sitting at a table shows the seats, your hand, and the round: who has played --
never what they played -- and the question being estimated.  Playing a card is
one click, changing your mind is another, and the card you hold is marked as
yours.  The cards stay face down until somebody presses *turn the cards over*,
and until then the page says only that a seat has played: a browser that asked
early is told nothing rather than told a smaller something, and the endpoint
behind the page has no field for a card in it at all.

Turning the cards over is where the conversation goes to.  Each seat shows the
card it played, and *what the room said* counts the table out: one bar per card,
the card the room agrees on -- or the two cards it is split between, because a
tie is not an agreement -- the range the votes ran from, whether everybody
played the same card, and the average.  Both decks are configurable: the ladders
are ~poker-decks~ in ~lib/lucidplan/domain/poker.scm~, and the pages, the labels
and the tally are all built from them.

Once the cards are over the table can be dealt the *next thing* to estimate, and
the round it settled is kept under *already estimated* -- the question and the
card the room agreed on -- so the record of the meeting is on the page rather
than in somebody's notes.  An item the room dealt but never turned over is shown
as unsettled rather than with the cards it was holding: a page about a meeting
has no business publishing a round the table never looked at.  *Closing a table*
takes it off the list of tables somebody could walk into, and keeps all of this
readable, so what it estimated and what it decided are not lost with it.

A table is the one page in LucidPlan several people look at at the same time, so
it keeps itself current.  It asks the room's own state endpoint every few seconds
and patches the seat marks in place -- a name changing from *waiting* to
*played*, and the count in the heading above them -- while a round that has moved
on or cards that have been turned over mean the page changes shape, so it is
re-read rather than patched.  The server is the only thing that decides how a
revealed round reads, so there is no second renderer to disagree with the first.

The one thing a table does that needs an account is writing its answer down.
Nobody at a table needs one to play a card, but the answer is only worth
something in the backlog, and a backlog is where accounts are.  So once the
cards are over and the room has agreed on one card, a reader whose account
holds ~lucidplan:ticket-create~ -- the same permission the ticket's own estimate
is changed with, and the same check the endpoint behind the panel makes, so the
control is drawn exactly when pressing it would be honoured -- is offered
*writing it on a ticket*: which project, and the ticket's number in it, unless
the table was opened for a ticket, in which case there is nothing to ask and the
push is one button naming it.  The
card then goes onto that ticket's estimate, on the ladder the room dealt -- a
table on the t-shirt deck writes the size and leaves the points alone, because a
push is not a reason to clear an estimate somebody else made -- and it leaves a
row in the ticket's own activity naming who wrote it and what the number was
before, exactly as the ticket's own page does.

A round the room split over has nothing to write, because a tie is not an
agreement, and neither has a round nobody played; the table is then dealt the
next thing and the round it settled keeps where its number went, as a link: the
meeting decides, the backlog is written down after it, and the page says which
of the two happened.

** How the pages treat a reader who is not signed in
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: how-the-pages-treat-a-reader-who-is-not-signed-in
:END:

LucidPlan is readable before it is writable. A signed-out reader can browse the
front page, the about page, every project's backlog and board, and each
ticket -- but every page that exists to submit or change something is behind the
permission that change actually needs, and turns the reader away *before* they
fill in a form that could only ever refuse them.

The pages that create or edit something ask for the same permission their write
does:

- creating or editing a project -- ~lucidplan:project-create~;
- creating or editing a ticket -- ~lucidplan:ticket-create~;
- managing users -- ~lucidplan:modify-permissions~.

The small inline controls on a ticket (its status, its type and its two
estimates) are shown to exactly the readers the endpoint behind them would
accept, so a reader who holds the permission sees the control and a reader who
does not sees the read-only badge.  The same holds for the rest of a ticket's
own page: the comment box, the upload form, the time form, the form that sets
the plan, the remove controls, the restore buttons and the menu that moves the
ticket to another project are all offered by the permission their write needs,
~lucidplan:ticket-create~ -- except for removing somebody else's comment or
somebody else's time entry, which is offered only to a reader holding
~lucidplan:ticket-delete~, the permission the endpoint checks as well.  The
sprint and milestone pages follow the same rule: everybody may read them, and
only a holder of ~lucidplan:ticket-create~ is offered the forms that write them.

** What the mail service is asked to send
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: what-the-mail-service-is-asked-to-send
:END:

An instance that has been given a [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/pingwing][pingwing]] address (~pingwing-url~ and its two
credentials, see [[#configuring-the-instance][Configuring the instance]]) writes to the people of a project when a
ticket is created in it -- the news that a board moved is worth having even when
nobody is looking at it.

Who hears it is everyone who takes part in the project: every user the project
has assigned a ticket to, together with the ticket's own assignee and the person
who opened it. LucidPlan keeps no membership list, so participation is read from
the work itself; a user with no email address is passed over, and an address is
written to once however many times it comes up.

The message is pingwing's ~lucidplan-new-ticket~ template, filled with the
project, the ticket's number and title, its type, its status as the project's own
board names it, its priority, the assignee and the author, the description, and a
link to the ticket on ~site-url~. The subject is the instance's own
~mail-subject-prefix~ and then ~New ticket in <project>~; the sender is
~mail-sender-name~ and ~mail-sender-address~.

Nothing about this can fail a write: a mail service that is unreachable, or that
refuses the task, is tried again a few times (~mail-retry-attempts~,
~mail-retry-delay~) and then given up on with a line in the log that repeats what
the service said, and the ticket is already saved either way. A message counts as
sent only when the service answers that it took it: a request that arrived and
was refused is a message that did not go, and is treated as one.

** Configuring the instance
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: configuring-the-instance
:END:

An instance keeps its private configuration in a module called ~(lucidplan env)~,
which LucidPlan loads at start-up from a file named ~env.scm~ in the root of the
project. That file is *per-deployment and must not be committed* (it is already
in ~.gitignore~). Create it:

#+begin_src scheme
(define-module (lucidplan env))

;; -- Secrets: unique to this deployment ------------------------------

;; Encrypts the session token. Generate a fresh UUID for each key.
(define-public encryption-key "a2ebe4c5-4a78-4490-af83-cfb05a285bf9")
(define-public signing-key "8d3a5bcc-973e-4f80-aa3d-33be33d6631c")

;; -- Mail: how password resets and invites are delivered ---------------
;; Pingwing is a mail and notification service written in Guile Scheme,
;; also part of the jointhefreeworld project. Find it at
;; https://codeberg.org/jjba23/pingwing -- without it, invites and
;; password resets cannot be delivered.
(define-public pingwing-url "https://pingwing.example.org")
(define-public pingwing-basic-auth-user "my-user")
(define-public pingwing-basic-auth-password "my-password")

;; -- Site: what this instance says about itself ------------------------
;; Every value below is optional; omit any line to keep LucidPlan's
;; default. See the table further down for the whole list.
(define-public site-name "LucidPlan")
(define-public site-url "https://lucidplan.example.org")
(define-public contact-email "admin@example.org")
(define-public copyright-holder "Your Name or Organisation")
#+end_src

Every key above is read by ~(lucidplan config)~ at start-up: a value defined in
the env module wins, and anything you leave out falls back to the default that
ships with LucidPlan. See [[#instance-configuration-keys][Instance configuration keys]] for the full list.

If you run LucidPlan locally, it is recommended to use ~localhost~ rather than
~127.0.0.1~, as this works better with the authentication, authorization and
other cookies.

** Renaming or changing the ticket statuses
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: renaming-or-changing-the-ticket-statuses
:END:

Which statuses a ticket moves through is configuration, not code: an instance
that does not use every status drops the ones it does not need, gives the ones
it keeps the names its team says, and adds any that are missing.

The key is ~ticket-statuses~, a list of entries. Each entry names one status and
means ~(value label icon glyph bucket)~:

- ~value~ -- what the database stores, e.g. ~"todo"~. Keep it stable: renaming a
  status means changing its ~label~;
- ~label~ -- what a reader is shown, e.g. ~"To-do"~;
- ~icon~ -- the name of a [[https://phosphoricons.com/][Phosphor icon]], e.g. ~"anchor"~;
- ~glyph~ -- one character, used in the menus a page cannot dress;
- ~bucket~ -- ~"open"~, ~"done"~ or ~"rejected"~. Open statuses are the board's
  columns and the backlog's live list; the closed ones become their own sections
  and their own counts.

So an instance whose team works in a speak-now-or-forever flow might say:

#+begin_src scheme
(define-public ticket-statuses
  '(("backlog" "Backlog" "tray" "📥" "open")
    ("doing" "Doing" "gear-fine" "⚙️" "open")
    ("review" "In Review" "list-magnifying-glass" "🔍" "open")
    ("blocked" "Blocked" "prohibit" "🚫" "open")
    ("shipped" "Shipped" "check" "✅" "done")
    ("wontfix" "Won't Fix" "x" "❌" "rejected")))
#+end_src

Two things follow from the list being the instance's:

- the *order* of the entries is the order of the board's columns and of every
  status menu;
- a status that is dropped from the list does not make its tickets disappear. A
  ticket carrying a value the list does not name is treated as open, so it stays
  on the board and can be dragged back onto a status that exists.

A status whose label is one of the shipped ones ([[#instance-configuration-keys][the list above]] keeps
~"To-do"~, ~"Work in Progress"~ and so on) is a translation key and is shown in
the reader's own language; a label of your own is shown exactly as written.

** A project with its own statuses
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: a-project-with-its-own-statuses
:END:

A single instance can host very different projects, and one team's board is not
always another's. So the instance's ~ticket-statuses~ is a *default*: any project
may override the whole list for itself, from its own edit page, in the same
one-status-to-a-line format:

#+name: status-labels
#+begin_example
todo | Backlog | anchor | ⚓ | open
work-in-progress | Doing | gear-fine | ⚙️ | open
done | Shipped | check | ✅ | done
#+end_example

The fields are ~value | label | icon | glyph | bucket~, in that order, and
everything but the value may be left out -- the value is then also the label, the
icon is a dot and the bucket is ~open~. A line beginning with ~#~ is a comment, so
the editor is also a place to leave a note. Leaving the field empty gives the
project back the instance's list.

Nothing else has to change: the project's backlog, its board, its ticket pages
and its status menus are all built from whichever list the project ends up with.

** The database
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: the-database
:END:

LucidPlan needs a database in the root folder of the project (alongside ~app~,
~lib~, etc.). It uses SQLite by default, and is also compatible with [[https://www.postgresql.org/][PostgreSQL]]
and [[https://www.mysql.com/][MySQL]].

If you use SQLite you should enable [[https://sqlite.org/wal.html][WAL mode]] for concurrent writes. The ~maak~
task does that for you (and creates the file):

#+begin_src sh
maak init-db
#+end_src

Schema migrations are managed with [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/mutastructura][mutastructura]], and they are applied with one task:

#+begin_src sh
maak migrate
#+end_src

~maak init-db~ makes a fresh database and migrates it in one go, so a new
instance never has to think about this. On a database that already exists,
~maak migrate~ applies whatever it has not seen and leaves the rest alone --
running it twice in a row is a no-op, and running it on a current database prints
~NOTHING TO DO!~.

A migration is a plain [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL][SQL]] file under ~resources/migrations/sqlite/~, named
~YYYY-MM-DD_HH:MM:SS_something.sql~: mutastructura applies the files it has not
seen in filename order, each in its own transaction, so a migration that fails
leaves the schema where it was rather than half-moved. What it has seen is kept
in a ~migrations_history~ table -- the ledger -- with the [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHA-2][SHA-256]] of each file,
so editing a migration that has already run is reported as a
~WARNING: Checksum mismatch~ instead of being applied a second time. The ledger
is the reason migrations are applied by ~maak migrate~ and not by hand: a
database migrated by hand has no ledger, and its schema can no longer be compared
with the migrations.

An existing deployment that was set up before LucidPlan used mutastructura has
one of those unledgered databases. Bring it up to date once, by hand, by
recording what is already applied -- the migration files are not re-run, and
nothing in the database changes:

#+begin_src sh
# Run this once, on an instance that was migrated by hand:
sqlite3 lucidplan.db "CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS migrations_history (
    id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT,
    name VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
    checksum VARCHAR(64) NOT NULL,
    applied_at DATETIME DEFAULT CURRENT_TIMESTAMP,
    UNIQUE (name, checksum))"
for f in resources/migrations/sqlite/*.sql; do
  sqlite3 lucidplan.db "INSERT OR IGNORE INTO migrations_history (name, checksum) VALUES (
      '$(basename "$f")',
      '$(sha256sum "$f" | cut -d' ' -f1)'))"
done
#+end_src

After that, ~maak migrate~ answers ~NOTHING TO DO!~ and every later migration is
applied by the one task.

** Creating the first super-user
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: creating-the-first-super-user
:END:

A brand-new LucidPlan has no users at all, and the user-management pages need a
user to reach them -- so the very first account is seeded directly into the
database, by hand.

A super-user is simply a user whose permission list contains
~lucidplan:modify-permissions~: that permission lets its holder grant every other
permission to themselves and to others, so it is the root of the whole ACL.

1. Pick a password and a salt (any string works; the salt just has to be kept
   with the account).
2. Compute the stored password, which is the *SHA-256 of =password + salt=*
   (password first, salt appended, no separator). You can compute it from the
   same shell you run the project in:

#+begin_src sh
# With the exact Guile the project uses (inside `guix shell -m manifest.scm`):
guile -c '(use-modules (artanis security nss)) (display (string->sha-256 (string-append "my-password" "my-personal-salt"))) (newline)'
#+end_src

3. Insert the row:

#+begin_src sql
INSERT INTO "user" (
    id,           -- any unique UUID for this account
    username,
    email,        -- the address you will sign in with
    password,     -- the SHA-256 computed above
    salt,         -- the salt you used above
    permissions   -- a back-quoted Lisp list of symbols
) VALUES (
    '2f6a4d3e-1c2b-4a5f-9e8d-0b7c6a5d4e3f',
    'Joe',
    'you@example.org',
    'the-sha256-you-computed',
    'my-personal-salt',
    '`(lucidplan:modify-permissions lucidplan:ticket-create lucidplan:ticket-delete lucidplan:project-create lucidplan:project-delete lucidplan:user-create lucidplan:user-delete)'
);
#+end_src

The ~permissions~ column is read back with Lisp's ~eval-string~, so it must be
the *text of a back-quoted list* -- that is, it starts with a backtick (`` ` ``)
and contains a Scheme list of the ~lucidplan:...~ symbols. The list above is the
full set; give the super-user all of them.

#+begin_src sh
sqlite3 lucidplan.db "INSERT INTO \"user\" (id, username, email, password, salt, permissions) VALUES ('2f6a4d3e-1c2b-4a5f-9e8d-0b7c6a5d4e3f','Joe','you@example.org','THE_SHA256','my-personal-salt','\`(lucidplan:modify-permissions lucidplan:ticket-create lucidplan:ticket-delete lucidplan:project-create lucidplan:project-delete lucidplan:user-create lucidplan:user-delete)');"
#+end_src

** Creating the other users
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: creating-the-other-users
:END:

Once you can sign in as the super-user, the rest of the team is created from the
interface, not from SQL:

1. Sign in at ~/login~ with the super-user you seeded.
2. Open *user management* (the ~/users/manage~ page, linked from your profile page
   at ~/me~).
3. Press *new user*, give the account a username and an email, tick the
   permissions it should hold, and create it.

A new account is created with *no password*. Instead, LucidPlan sends its owner an
email containing a one-time link (a password reset) with which they set their own
password. An account is therefore created by the operator and *claimed* by its
owner -- the operator never chooses somebody else's password.

For that email to arrive, the mail service (~pingwing-url~ and its credentials,
above) has to be configured and reachable. If a message could not be delivered,
the account still exists and the invite can be sent again: on the user-management
page every account has a *resend invite* button, and the page tells you plainly
when a message failed to go out. Messages are also retried a few times before
giving up.

To give a single person the ability to manage users, tick
~lucidplan:modify-permissions~ for them. To let someone create and edit tickets
but nothing else, tick only ~lucidplan:ticket-create~.

** Host name, TLS and search engines
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: host-name-tls-and-search-engines
:END:

For a public instance, point ~host.name~ (in ~conf/production.conf~) at your
domain and put LucidPlan behind a reverse proxy that terminates [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security][TLS]], since the
session cookie is set ~secure~ and will not be stored over plain HTTP. Set
~site-url~ in ~env.scm~ to the public address so the canonical links and preview
images are absolute.

Search-engine basics are handled for you: every page carries a title, a
description, a canonical address and Open Graph/Twitter preview tags, plus a
[[https://json-ld.org/][JSON-LD]] block describing the application. ~/robots.txt~ is generated and points
at a ~/sitemap.xml~ that lists the public pages (the front page, the about page,
the project list, and each project's backlog and board).

** The theme, and the files it is made of
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: the-theme-and-the-files-it-is-made-of
:END:

LucidPlan wears a theme it serves itself. The Lucida faces, the two web font
families the stylesheet names after them, the Phosphor icon face, the stylesheets
that describe them, and the image a link preview shows are all files of this
project, under ~pub/css/~, ~pub/fonts/~ and ~pub/img/~. A page load therefore asks
this instance for them and no other host: nothing about your visitors is sent to
a [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_delivery_network][CDN]] or a font service, and nothing about your instance is told to anybody you
did not choose.

The files are committed, so a fresh checkout serves them without any extra step.
They are refreshed from where they came from by one task, which names each source:

#+begin_src sh
maak fetch-assets
#+end_src

Where the instance hands them out is configuration. Left alone, a page names them
by path -- ~/css/fonts.css~ -- and the instance that served the page answers for
them, which is what a self-hosted deployment wants. Set ~asset-url~ in ~env.scm~
to an absolute base to hand that work to a static host or a CDN instead:

#+begin_src scheme
(define-public asset-url "https://assets.example.org/lucidplan")
#+end_src

Page analytics is also yours to decide. The instance's own page-view script
reports to ~analytics-endpoint~, and setting it empty means the script is not put
on the page at all -- no request leaves the reader's browser:

#+begin_src scheme
(define-public analytics-endpoint "")
#+end_src

** Tests
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: tests
:END:

Tests are run with [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/veritas][veritas]]:

#+begin_src sh
maak test
#+end_src

They live under ~test/veritas/~: ~unit/~ for the tests that run on every change,
and ~benchmark/~ for those that measure rather than assert. A test is a module
like any other and is loaded with the application's own directories on the path,
so it can ~use-modules~ what it tests. Every push to the trunk branch runs the
suite on [[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/byggsteg][byggsteg]] before anything is deployed: =GitHub Actions= is asked for that
job by [[./.github/workflows/ci.yml][.github/workflows/ci.yml]], which calls
[[https://github.com/jjba23/byggsteg-github][byggsteg-github]]'s reusable workflow
rather than carrying the steps in this repository. The job is the same three
tasks as it has always been -- ~maak-test~, ~pull-and-restart~ and
~maak-deploy~ -- run by the daemon that file names, and the build goes green or
red according to what the deployment said it did.

** Translations
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: translations
:END:

The interface is translated with [[https://www.gnu.org/software/gettext/][gettext]]. The catalogs live under
~sys/i18n/locale/~. The languages offered today are English (US), Nederlands,
Español and 日本語. When you add translatable strings (they are written as
~(G_ "...")~), refresh and compile the catalogs:

#+begin_src sh
maak i18n-update-template   # collect new strings into the .pot template
maak i18n-merge             # merge the template into every .po
maak i18n-compile           # compile the .po files into the .mo the server reads
#+end_src

The languages offered in the interface are configuration: set ~locales~ in
~env.scm~ to the catalogs you actually have.

* Authentication, Authorization, User management
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: authentication-authorization-user-management
:END:

[[https://codeberg.org/jjba23/bewaking][bewaking]] 🔗 is a nice library to help you secure your web applications with
Guile Scheme.

With bewaking we use secure tokens encrypted and stored in secure cookies (for
browsers) and headers (for REST API) as well as authentication and authorization,
with ACLs (access control list) and fine-grained permissions.

If you want to be a super user/root user in lucidplan your token's permissions
list will need to contain the ~lucidplan:modify-permissions~ permission,
effectively meaning you can give yourself and others all wanted permissions and
do everything.

LucidPlan's permission model ACL:
- ~lucidplan:ticket-create~
- ~lucidplan:ticket-delete~
- ~lucidplan:project-create~
- ~lucidplan:project-delete~
- ~lucidplan:user-create~
- ~lucidplan:user-delete~
- ~lucidplan:modify-permissions~

LucidPlan requires an authenticated session for performing administrative tasks
such as creating/deleting tickets and projects. For web browsers you can simply
use the login page; for scripts and headless usage, use the ~/api/v1/login~
endpoint and take the ~x-bewaking-lucidplan~ response header.

** The REST API
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: the-rest-api
:END:

The versioned API lives under ~/api/v1~ and speaks [[https://www.json.org/][JSON]]. All of it is guarded by
the same ACL as the pages, so a token carries exactly the permissions its owner
has.

|--------------------------+------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| *Method and path*        | *Answer*                     | *Notes*                                   |
|--------------------------+------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| ~POST /api/v1/login~     | ~204~ and the token, or ~401~ | Body: ~email~ and ~password~, URL-encoded |
| ~GET /api/v1/projects~   | the project list             | Token header needed                       |
| ~GET /api/v1/tickets/:project~ | a project's tickets    | ~:project~ is the project's human id      |
|--------------------------+------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|

#+begin_src sh
# Sign in and keep the token the service answers with:
TOKEN=$(curl -s -D - -o /dev/null -X POST \
  -d 'email=you@example.org&password=your-password' \
  http://localhost:50025/api/v1/login | awk '/[Xx]-bewaking-lucidplan/{print $2}' | tr -d '\r')

# Ask for a project's tickets with it:
curl -s -H "x-bewaking-lucidplan: $TOKEN" \
  http://localhost:50025/api/v1/tickets/lucidplan
#+end_src

** On Reproducibility: Why GNU Guix?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: on-reproducibility-why-gnu-guix
:END:

A build should never fail because "it worked on my machine." Reliable software
development requires explicit, bit-for-bit reproducibility and a functional
approach to package management and system deployment.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT html
<a href="https://guix.gnu.org/">
  <img src="https://jointhefreeworld.org/ggg/dist/gnu-guix.svg" alt="GNU Guix logo" style="max-width: 250px; width: 100%; height: auto;" />
</a>
#+END_EXPORT

- *Declarative Build Graphs:* Packages are pure functions of their inputs with no implicit system state.
- *Programmable with Guile:* Full system and package configurations written in [[https://www.gnu.org/software/guile/][Guile Scheme]] rather than rigid DSLs or static files.
- *Time Travel:* Rebuild the exact system state from any point in [[https://git-scm.com/][git]] history using ~guix time-machine~.
- *Atomic Operations:* Instantly upgrade or roll back environments without leaving orphaned dependencies or broken states.
- *Unified Tooling:* One tool manages development shells, isolated containers, package builds, and full system configurations.

** On GNU Artanis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: on-gnu-artanis
:END:

Find the documentation for Artanis here: https://www.gnu.org/software/artanis/manual/

GNU Artanis is the first production-level modern Web framework of the Scheme programming language.

It is designed and maintained to be robust, fast, and easy to use for professional web development.

Here lies not just a framework, but a canvas where your ideas take flight with remarkable speed and grace. Artanis isn't about wrestling with complexity; it's about the joy of creation.

It's about harnessing the power of a robust and mature language to build something truly your own, something beautiful, nimble, flexible, powerful, efficient, understandable and maintainable. GNU Artanis was Certificated as Awesome Project at 2013 Lisp in summer projects.

One day, the folks at GNU were discussing what language they would write the GNU website in - and many chose [[https://www.python.org/][Python]]. But I found that strange, because the official extension language of GNU is GNU Guile. And I wondered aloud - why not start a brand new project to provide a web framework written with GNU Guile? To which [[https://stallman.org/][RMS]] said, "It's cool, I like this idea."

But at that time, it was just an idea without a plan.

Fortunately, a few months later, the Guile community held a hack-potluck to celebrate Guile2 turning two - which is a contest to write a cool program in a few weeks. And so, Artanis was born.

History:

    February 2013 - Artanis born at the GNU Guile hack-potluck.
    2013 - Artanis submitted to "Lisp In Summer Projects" contest. Received "Certificated awesome project" award in 2014.
    1st January, 2015 - the first stable version Artanis-0.0.1 was released.
    19th January, 2015 - offers Artanis to [[https://www.fsf.org/][FSF]]/GNU, and RMS inducts it as an official GNU project. Artanis becomes GNU Artanis.
    29 December 2021 - GNU Artanis donates to HardenedLinux community to connect with product environment more tightly.
